Template to Temple

The artwork on this store is commissioned-grade. The store around it is a builder template with the defaults still on. Closing that gap is the upgrade — and most of it is a weekend of work, not a rebuild.

The gap

Your art is premium. Your chrome is stock.

Look at what a visitor actually processes: a magnificent gold-and-chrome tree of life, then a default sans-serif nav, a rounded amber button, and a three-column grid with builder-default type. The eye reads the mismatch instantly, even when the viewer can't name it — and the conclusion it reaches is this is an artist who uploaded good art to a template, not this is a collector's storefront.

That perception is what makes $299 feel risky. Nothing below asks you to change the artwork. It asks you to extract a design system out of the artwork you already paid for, and apply it to every surface the art isn't covering.

Move one — tokens

Pull the palette out of the album cover

You have two visual worlds already and they're both strong: KhemisTree is gold, chrome and obsidian; VLTRN is electric blue, plasma orange and nebula purple. Right now neither is defined anywhere in the site's CSS, so every button and heading falls back to a builder default. Define these once as variables and the entire site snaps into alignment.

Keep the split disciplined: gold is the store, blue-orange is the record. Gold does all the chrome — buttons, rules, badges, nav. VLTRN's colors appear only on the release pages and product imagery. That way the next project gets its own palette without you rebuilding the site, and the parent brand stays constant underneath.

Three typefaces, three jobs

One face doing everything is the loudest signal of a template. Split the work: a high-contrast serif for display (it echoes the metallic KhemisTree logotype), a plain grotesque for reading, and a mono for anything that behaves like data — prices, badges, countdown, shipping windows, size specs.

Move two — hero

Let the art be the stage, not the whole play

Right now the artwork is the page — full viewport, no type over it, the button stranded underneath. That's a poster, and posters don't sell; they get admired and scrolled past. The fix is layering rather than stacking.

  • Crop the art to a band, roughly 60–70vh, not the full screen.
  • Lay a gradient scrim over the lower third — obsidian at 85% fading to transparent — so type sits on it legibly without dimming the image.
  • Set the headline and buttons on top of the art, bottom-left. The CTA is visible without scrolling, which is the whole point.
  • Keep the KhemisTree mark small in the header. A logo at full-screen scale reads as a splash page, and splash pages are a decade out of date.

The same pattern carries the VLTRN page: cover art as the ground, release date and pre-order button layered over it, tracklist and credits below the fold.

Move four — art direction

Replace the infographics with photography

This is the single biggest visual upgrade available to you, and it's the one that isn't code. Your three bundle images are sales flyers — dense, text-packed, built for a feed. Your six merch images are clean product renders. Side by side in one grid, the inconsistency is what makes the store read as improvised.

Once the bundle facts live in HTML (Move three), those graphics have no job left. Give every SKU the same treatment instead:

  • One ground, one light setup, one aspect ratio across all nine. Square, shot on obsidian, single warm key light from the upper left to catch the gold — matched to your own brand lighting.
  • Bundles as flat lays — the actual cassette, hat and tee arranged together. A flat lay sells a bundle far better than a diagram of one, because it shows the haul.
  • One worn shot per apparel item. Nobody buys a $99 jersey from a floating render. This is also the cheapest fix on the list.
  • A second image on hover — back of the jersey, the embroidery detail, the vinyl out of the sleeve.

Keep the flyer graphics. They're good — they just belong on Instagram, where a dense promo image is the right format, not in a product grid.

Move six — motion

One gesture, used sparingly

Your brand is metallic, so the motion should be too: a slow specular sweep across the gold on button hover — a highlight travelling left to right over about 600ms. That single gesture, reused on buttons and badges, reads as deliberate craft.

Everything else stays still. A quiet fade-in on the product grid as it enters the viewport, and nothing more. Resist parallax, floating particles and animated backgrounds — heavy motion is the fastest way to make an expensive-looking site feel cheap, and it wrecks performance on the in-app browsers most of your traffic arrives through. Wrap all of it in prefers-reduced-motion.

The bigger question

Is the platform itself the ceiling?

Several problems here aren't design problems — they're limits of the builder the store sits on. Proper product pages with variants, size charts, abandoned-cart email, review capture, a pre-order counter, bundle logic and inventory caps on the vinyl are all standard on a commerce-native platform and all awkward-to-impossible on a general site builder.

My read: do the design work first, on the platform you're on. Moves one through four are worth doing regardless and none of them are wasted in a migration — tokens, copy, photography and hierarchy all transfer intact. Then decide on the platform after this release, using real numbers, and never mid-campaign. Migrating a live pre-order is how you lose orders and break links at exactly the moment attention peaks.

Sequence

Build order

Ordered so the cheapest work with the largest visible effect lands first.

Weekend 1

Tokens and type

Define the palette and the three-face type scale. Restyle nav, buttons, headings and prices against them. Nothing structural — but this alone removes most of the template smell.

Weekend 1

Hero rebuild

Crop the art, add the scrim, set headline and two buttons over it. Shrink the logo into the header.

Week 2

Product card + comparison table

Move every fact out of the JPEGs into text. Add badges, anchor pricing and the tier comparison. This is where conversion actually moves.

Week 2

Countdown, sticky bar, audio

Three small components, each removing a specific reason people leave without buying.

Weeks 3–4

Photography

One shoot, nine SKUs, one lighting setup, plus worn shots for the three apparel items. The longest lead time here, and the biggest jump in perceived value.

After the drop

Platform decision

Revisit with real conversion data in hand. Not before, and not during the campaign.
